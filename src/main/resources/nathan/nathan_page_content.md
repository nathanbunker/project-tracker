I built Dandelion Daily to solve that problem for myself.

Dandelion Daily is one of the experiments that stayed.

## Measurement & Improvement

I identified that public health immunization systems claiming to follow the same national standard behaved very differently in practice. Early measurement work with the Indian Health Service produced evidence that changed the national understanding of the problem and helped lead to a major CDC-funded improvement program at AIRA. I later joined AIRA as the first technical hire for the initiative and helped transfer the early project into an organizational capability that could scale.

## Aggregate Analysis Reporting Tool (AART)

I designed and built the platform that transformed measurement from a labor-intensive service into a scalable operational program. AART became the technical backbone of AIRA's Measurement & Improvement work and supported a multidisciplinary national improvement effort. The standardization work proved especially valuable when immunization systems had to absorb dramatically increased reporting volumes during the COVID-19 response.

## Emerging Standards & InteropHub

I created and positioned a program to help the immunization community move from a mature legacy standard toward newer approaches such as FHIR. I developed the topic and facilitation model and then began building InteropHub to make the work more durable, visible, scalable, and less dependent on a single facilitator.

## International Vaccine Codes

I formed an international initiative to address inconsistent vaccine coding across countries and systems. The work has involved partners in the United States, France, Canada, the Netherlands, and elsewhere, including an in-person working meeting in Bordeaux. It is an example of work that has continued to develop despite limited institutional funding because the underlying problem remains worth solving.

## Dandelion Daily

A multi-project productivity and time-management application grounded in practical project tracking and Getting Things Done principles. I originally built it for my own work across many concurrent projects. It became central to how I manage commitments and is now being prepared for wider use through Dandelion Software & Research.

## InteropHub

A community and project coordination platform designed to organize emerging standards, participation, decisions, and progress across distributed technical groups.

## IIS Sandbox

A reference implementation used to test and demonstrate immunization information exchange.

## Lone Star Forecaster

A stand-alone application that evaluates an immunization history and returns recommendations about vaccinations that are complete or due next for a patient.

## Step Into CDSi

## 1. Serve the Client. Build the Field.

### Short summary

A consulting assignment for the Indian Health Service looked narrow: help local healthcare facilities connect to state immunization systems. Working closely enough on that immediate problem exposed a much larger national one. Systems that were supposed to implement the same standard behaved differently, and nobody had been measuring the difference.

The larger program that eventually emerged did not begin with a strategic initiative or a funding proposal. It began by solving the client's problem carefully enough to see the pattern underneath it.

**Lesson:** Do the work the client needs now, but stay close enough to the operational problem to recognize what the whole field may need next.

> **Display note:** Show the short summary first. The following full story should be collapsed or otherwise secondary until the reader chooses to continue.

### Full narrative

Vaccination is one of the great successes of public health. It has prevented millions of deaths and vastly more episodes of serious illness, and it makes modern, highly connected societies safer from diseases that once moved routinely through populations.

Yet public health operates with surprisingly limited resources. In healthcare IT, public health is a small fish among much larger organizations and industries. When public health succeeds, very little happens: people do not become sick, outbreaks do not spread, and systems quietly continue functioning. That makes success easy to overlook and sustained investment difficult.

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated the pattern dramatically. When widespread illness threatened the economy and healthcare system, enormous resources suddenly flowed into public health. As the immediate crisis receded, much of that funding disappeared just as agencies were beginning to modernize systems that had been neglected for years.

Funding is only part of the difficulty. Public-health projects operate across government agencies, healthcare organizations, IT departments, legislatures, federal funders, contractors, vendors, legal requirements, and changing political priorities. Nobody simply decides that a good technical idea should happen. Progress usually requires many independent organizations to choose to cooperate.

That was the environment in which electronic immunization reporting emerged.

During the 2000s and early 2010s, healthcare providers were rapidly replacing paper records with electronic health records. State and jurisdictional Immunization Information Systems had traditionally depended heavily on manual data entry. Once vaccinations were already being entered electronically at the point of care, an obvious question emerged: why should someone enter them again into a public-health website?

The technical answer was HL7 messaging. The immunization community came together and developed a common implementation guide so healthcare systems could send vaccination records electronically to public health.

Then federal incentives accelerated EHR adoption. Healthcare systems receiving those incentives had to demonstrate that their immunization interfaces actually worked. They could not simply say they followed the standard.

Public-health immunization systems were treated differently.

The IIS community had helped create the standard. Federal funding was provided to help programs modernize their interfaces. Everyone had every incentive to get it right. The reasonable assumption was that if an IIS said it implemented the national guide, it did.

There was just one problem.

Nobody was testing them.

Around this time, I was consulting for the Indian Health Service. The assignment sounded fairly narrow. IHS needed help connecting its local healthcare facilities to state and jurisdictional Immunization Information Systems, the public health systems that collect vaccination records.

I had worked with IHS before while employed by an IIS software vendor. For this project, I developed an application that could take information from IHS's legacy file-based systems and send it to an IIS using the national messaging standard. The software itself did not take especially long to build.

The difficult part was making each connection work.

A single interface could involve the local IHS clinic, an IHS area office, the central IHS information technology department, the vendor producing the outgoing data, the receiving IIS, and the IIS software vendor. If any participant did not understand the next step, lacked the necessary information, or had competing priorities, the project stopped moving.

This placed me in an unusually useful position. I already understood how IIS interfaces were built because I had written one of the major interface products used in the field. I knew what happened inside the receiving systems. Now I was seeing the same transactions from the sender's perspective.

I had seen how the sausage was made on both sides.

IHS needed to connect with approximately 35 different IIS. In theory, this should have been manageable because they were all supposed to follow the same national implementation guide.

In practice, every connection was different.

IHS could see the variation, but it did not always understand why the receiving systems behaved differently. The IIS community understood its own systems, but it did not fully appreciate what those variations looked like to an organization trying to connect across the country.

This was not only an IHS problem. Electronic health record systems everywhere were beginning to face the same challenge. At the time, only a small portion of immunization information was being submitted electronically. Over the following decade, that proportion would grow from roughly 5 percent to approximately 95 percent.

I had a front-row seat to what would become a national operational problem.

My immediate responsibility was still to IHS. I needed to build the connection software, troubleshoot interfaces, answer questions, and help individual projects move forward. But the client's short-term need and the field's long-term need were aligned. If IIS followed the shared standard more consistently, IHS would benefit directly.

The work therefore had two levels:

Help IHS connect today.

Help the community understand why those connections were unnecessarily difficult.

During this period, I worked for several organizations at the same time. That was possible because the public health community was small, collaborative, and accustomed to sharing lessons. I was open about whom I worked for, and clients often hired me precisely because I could bring knowledge from elsewhere in the field.

That did not mean client boundaries were unimportant. Precise time tracking was essential. I developed and used Dandelion Daily to record my work closely and make sure each client received the attention and value for which it was paying.

The larger ethical principle was straightforward: protect confidential information, serve the client's interests, and share what the community needed to learn.

Public health interoperability did not improve by keeping every failure private. It improved when organizations could discuss recurring problems honestly and learn from one another.

I eventually brought the variation problem to the board of the American Immunization Registry Association. Not everyone was convinced.

One response was that electronic health record companies simply did not want to support public health requirements. Perhaps complaints about inconsistent IIS behavior were excuses from companies trying to avoid the work.

I did not yet have enough data to refute that argument. But I knew what IHS was experiencing. IHS was not a profit-seeking software company attempting to escape its responsibilities. It was a federal healthcare system trying earnestly to connect with state public health programs, and those connections were still extremely difficult.

The issue eventually traveled through an unexpected path. A colleague working with the IHS project was asked to explain why connections with state IIS were so difficult. By then, we had documented the differences we were encountering. Her explanation moved up through federal leadership and eventually returned to the CDC program responsible for working with IIS.

It was an uncomfortable moment. CDC had been told that IIS were creating significant problems for IHS, and the people responsible for the IIS program had not realized the scale of the issue.

But it also created an opening.

CDC brought me in to expand the limited analysis we had done for IHS into a one-time national status check. We tested more than half of the IIS in the United States, with strong participation from systems that were available for testing.

When I present this history, I sometimes ask people how many systems we found that fully followed the national standard.

People often guess half.

The answer was none.

Not one of the systems we tested fully implemented the guide as written.

The important lesson was not that public health organizations were careless or unwilling. Most believed they were following the standard. The real problem was that we had published a guide and assumed publication would produce consistency.

There had been no common testing process. No reliable measurement. No way for an IIS to see how its implementation differed from the guide or from its peers.

Everyone was trying to do the right thing, but we had not given them the feedback necessary to succeed.

Delivering that result required more than technical skill. IIS programs had to trust us enough to provide access to their systems. They had to believe that the measurements were intended to help rather than embarrass them. They also had to be willing to hear difficult news.

They were.

Once programs could see the differences clearly, they began changing voluntarily. No one had to order them to conform. Most did not want to be different. They simply needed credible information showing where the differences were.

The one-time status check led CDC to recognize that this required an ongoing national effort. Funding was extended to AIRA and increased over time. I joined the organization as its first technical hire for what became the Measurement and Improvement initiative.

I brought software, technical experience, and the original measurement work. But I did not build the resulting program alone. Talented colleagues at AIRA shaped the initial idea into a mature improvement process, worked directly with IIS programs, refined the measures, and built a lasting national capability.

Over many years, the IIS community moved toward far greater consistency. That work became particularly important during the COVID-19 pandemic, when immunization systems had to accept unprecedented volumes of data from many new sources. The infrastructure was not perfect, but years of standardization work had made it substantially more resilient.

Consulting requires holding two ideas at the same time.

The first is practical: do the work the client needs now.

The second is visionary: continue looking for the underlying problem and the better future that could exist.

The visionary goal does not excuse failing to deliver on the immediate assignment. The immediate assignment should provide the evidence, relationships, and practical understanding needed to pursue the larger goal responsibly.

I did not begin by seeking funding for a national improvement program. I began by helping IHS connect its systems. The larger opportunity emerged because I stayed close to the difficult operational work, documented what I saw, and kept asking what would make the entire environment function better.

My lesson is simple:

Funding may follow. A program may form. Other people may eventually take over and improve what you started.

But first, wash the dishes that need washing.

Go where the work is difficult and useful. Solve the problem in front of you, but keep watching for the larger pattern.

## 2. Earn Authority Without Power

### Short summary

AIRA could not order state immunization systems to participate in national measurement, change their software, or follow a common implementation approach. The work had to succeed without that kind of power.

That constraint changed the program. Participation had to be useful. Measurements had to be credible. Bad results had to help rather than merely embarrass. Standards had to become something the community increasingly owned.

**Lesson:** Power can require compliance. Authority is voluntarily granted. Durable technical change often depends on earning the second rather than possessing the first.

> **Display note:** Show the short summary first. The following full story should be collapsed or otherwise secondary until the reader chooses to continue.

### Full narrative

For a long time, I thought about power and authority as if they were almost the same thing. They are not.

Power is what allows someone to impose a consequence. A police officer can write a ticket. A parent can put a child in timeout. A supervisor can fire an employee. A federal agency can attach conditions to funding or decide that future funds will go somewhere else. The person on the receiving end may disagree, but the consequence does not disappear merely because they disagree with it.

Authority is different. A physician may tell you that you need to make a difficult change, but the physician usually cannot force you to do it. You follow the advice because you believe the person understands your problem, knows something you do not, and is trying to help you. The same thing happens when an adult asks a trusted parent for advice, or when a colleague has enough experience that people stop and listen when that person speaks. Authority is voluntarily granted.

The distinction became clearer to me through our work at AIRA. Immunization Information Systems are operated independently by states, cities, and other jurisdictions. AIRA does not regulate them, does not provide their operating funding, and cannot order them to change their systems. When we began measuring interoperability, we had essentially no mechanism for forcing anyone to participate. If the program was going to work, IIS programs had to believe there was a reason to participate.

At the time, that did not feel like a philosophical choice. It was simply the situation we were in.

The first measurements were intentionally easy to join. We had built automated tests that could run against an IIS test interface. To participate, an IIS generally needed to give us the address of its test system and credentials. We would run the tests and send the results back.

I remember one meeting where an IIS arrived ready to begin the process. They had assembled what seemed like everyone who might possibly be needed: a project manager, technical staff, operational staff, people prepared to coordinate the work. They had clearly expected a substantial government-style engagement. Someone would define a project plan, assignments would be made, forms would be completed, meetings would be scheduled, and perhaps sometime over the next year the organization would emerge with a result.

Instead, we asked for their test credentials.

Someone said that Bob could send those.

We said great. We would run the tests and send them the results.

There was a moment of almost bewildered silence. Was that really it?

It was.

That experience stayed with me because it illustrated something we had stumbled into. Since we could not compel participation, we had to be careful about the burden we placed on people. If we asked an IIS to spend time on something, the work needed to be useful. As the program expanded, not every measurement could be automated. Patient matching, for example, requires IIS staff to review information we cannot see ourselves. That takes hours. Yet participation has remained strong because patient matching is already a difficult problem for these programs. The measurement gives them information about something they genuinely want to understand. The time is not merely the cost of complying with a requirement; the process itself is useful.

The same principle gradually changed how people thought about receiving bad results.

At first, many IIS were understandably nervous about being measured. Some chose not to share their results with peers. I used to think of it as a junior high dance. Everyone is worried about how they look on the dance floor, while everyone else is mostly worried about themselves. In the early years, a substantial minority preferred to keep their results private. Over time that changed. Most IIS now share their results openly, and every year at our national meeting we hold what we call AART clinics. Staff sit down with individual programs, review their results, look at what has improved, identify what remains difficult, and talk about what help would be useful. It feels less like an inspection than a yearly checkup.

One episode changed my own understanding of what a measurement program could be.

An IIS we were testing had an interface that could receive immunization updates, but it returned a positive acknowledgment whether or not the message had actually been processed successfully. If something was wrong, the system created an internal IT ticket and a person followed up manually. Hundreds of tickets could be generated this way. The IIS staff member responsible for the program knew this was a serious limitation and had been trying to get several internal IT groups to devote resources to modernizing the interface.

Our initial report told her that the system was doing very well.

Technically, our software was behaving correctly. We sent a message, received a positive acknowledgment, and interpreted that acknowledgment according to the standard: the transaction had succeeded. The problem was that the acknowledgment itself could not be trusted.

She objected strongly to the report. It was not enough for us to put a caveat somewhere saying that the results were probably misleading. She was trying to demonstrate internally that the interface needed investment. If we handed her leadership a report full of green indicators, we would undermine the very improvements she was trying to make.

We had built a measurement system with the assumption that programs would prefer good scores. Here was an IIS insisting that we tell them they were failing.

So we changed the test. Before evaluating the rest of an interface, we began sending a message that no reasonable IIS should accept, such as one lacking information required to identify the patient. A conformant system should reject it. If the IIS still returned a positive acknowledgment, we knew the remaining results could not be trusted. Instead of showing success, we could show that the interface was not reliably testable.

The negative report was more useful to the program than the positive one.

That was an important shift. We had worried that measurement would embarrass IIS programs. What we began to see instead was that an accurate measurement could give them leverage inside their own organizations. A poor result was not necessarily an accusation. It could document a capability they knew they lacked and give them something concrete to take to their IT department, leadership, contractor, or budget office.

Of course, this only worked if the measures themselves were credible.

To develop them, AIRA created the Measurement for Assessment & Certification Advisory Workgroup, which we called MACAW. We invited people from across the community, including IIS programs, implementers, CDC, IHS, and others involved in the work. AIRA staff would develop a proposal, MACAW would critique it, we would revise it, and the group would critique it again. Once the proposal was mature enough, it went to the broader community.

We asked members to do something that is harder than it sounds. They were not there merely to defend what their own IIS or company already did. We asked them to put on the hat of the national community and think about what all IIS ought eventually to be able to do. That meant someone could support a standard that their own system did not yet meet.

This became one of the most important changes in the program. A measure could describe where the community wanted to go without pretending everyone was already there. We were not asking people to say, “This is exactly how I would have designed it.” The more useful question was, “Can you live with this, and can you support the community moving in this direction?”

That distinction mattered because the IIS community was not starting from the same place. Some systems had been exchanging electronic data for many years. They had mature interfaces, established workflows, and considerable investment in the way they already operated. From their perspective, standardization could look less like progress and more like being asked to abandon something that already worked.

Before I joined AIRA as an employee, I organized a meeting of roughly thirty people to work through some of these questions. I had called participants individually the week before so I could understand their positions and prepare the discussion. One participant represented a mature IIS and was considerably more forceful than most people in our community. He was experienced, confident, and accustomed to getting his way.

The week before the meeting, I explained the process to him and he seemed comfortable with it. At the meeting, he announced that he did not like the process and wanted it changed.

He may even have had legitimate criticisms. There is no perfect way to facilitate a difficult discussion. But changing the structure after the meeting had begun, in response to the most forceful person in the room, would have given him control over everyone else's participation. I told him we were going to continue with the process we had established.

Later, the discussion reached the question that mattered most: should IIS across the country be expected to move toward the same standard?

He said no. His position was understandable. Some IIS had been doing electronic exchange successfully for years. Why should a mature system that was already working be expected to change merely so that everyone else could be more similar?

Then the room went quiet.

People in public health tend to be agreeable. Reputations matter enormously because the same people work together for years, often across different projects and organizations. Open confrontation carries a cost. In this case, the silence did not feel like agreement to me. It felt like thirty people trying very hard not to have an argument with someone who was much more comfortable having one.

I called on a colleague from IHS. I knew what she had experienced trying to connect one national healthcare system to dozens of IIS, and I asked her a question that gave her room to explain it. She was visibly nervous, but she described what the differences meant from the other side of the transaction. Every supposedly local variation became another special case that IHS had to understand, build around, test, and maintain.

After she spoke, I asked the room directly whether, in principle, the community should expect IIS to move toward a common national standard.

Nearly every hand went up.

Then I asked who believed mature IIS should be allowed to continue using their own approaches rather than being encouraged to standardize.

One hand went up.

It was an uncomfortable moment. I did not enjoy putting someone in that position, and I do not believe minority opinions should be suppressed merely because they are unpopular. He had every right to argue that mature IIS should be left alone. In fact, the community needed to hear that argument.

What could not continue was allowing one person's willingness to dominate the discussion to stand in for everyone else's consent.

The meeting did not produce total agreement. It did something more useful: it made the direction of the community visible. From then on, we could have disagreements about the details while starting from a much clearer premise that national consistency was something most IIS actually wanted.

Over the following years, that premise became increasingly normal. We made participation itself visible. At first, simply agreeing to be measured was progress worth recognizing. Then we created basic and more complete levels of capability. Programs began passing measures they had once failed. We recognized improvement at national meetings and showed maps of participation and progress. There was peer pressure in that, certainly, but much of it was positive. Programs wanted to be part of a community that was moving forward.

The standard we eventually converged around was not perfect. No standard ever is. What mattered was that we had moved from a collection of individually reasonable interfaces toward a national infrastructure that could increasingly be depended upon. A handful of extremely sophisticated systems were less valuable to the country than a large number of systems that behaved consistently enough that healthcare organizations could connect to them without reinventing the interface every time.

By the 2020s, I noticed that an old criticism had begun to lose its force. During the previous decade, people routinely complained that every IIS was different. Later, when someone said that, we could ask which IIS they were talking about. Sometimes they had encountered a real problem, and we knew about it too. But one problematic system no longer described the entire field. We had enough measurement and enough shared experience to distinguish between a local problem and a national one.

That became particularly important during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Immunization systems suddenly had to absorb vastly more traffic and connect with many new data submitters. The infrastructure was far from perfect, but it handled an extraordinary increase in volume. Years of work aligning interfaces had quietly created capacity that became indispensable when the country needed it.

Only later did I begin thinking explicitly about the distinction between power and authority. AIRA had never been in a position to force IIS programs to participate in this work. We could not threaten them with lost funding, order them to change their software, or make conformance a condition of operation. We had to persuade them that the measurement was meaningful, make participation worth their time, listen when our methods were wrong, and create a process in which the standards increasingly belonged to the community rather than to us.

That is a slower way to lead.

But it produces something power alone cannot.

Eventually, people begin following not because they have to, but because they believe the direction is worth following.

## 3. Build More Ideas. Own Them Less.

### Short summary

I have always generated many ideas and tended to make them concrete quickly enough to discover what is wrong with them. Some have become important. Many have not. Success can make it easy to remember the surviving ideas and forget all the discarded ones.

Over time I have become less interested in proving that a particular idea is mine and more interested in producing enough possibilities that good ones can emerge, be criticized, change ownership, and eventually outgrow me.

**Lesson:** Have more ideas, test them sooner, become less attached to each one, and let successful work become something other people can change and own.

> **Display note:** Show the short summary first. The following full story should be collapsed or otherwise secondary until the reader chooses to continue.

### Full narrative

I have always had a lot of ideas, and they have never been limited to work. I was a vegetarian for years and now I am not. At one point I became interested in peak oil and preparedness and spent a fair amount of time thinking about what would happen if the systems we depended on stopped working. I learned French and Spanish and traveled around the world. My wife and I homeschooled our children. I got interested in sourdough. Long before Airbnb made staying in a stranger's house seem ordinary, we participated in Couchsurfing and hosted more than a hundred families in our home. Some people we knew thought this was crazy and wondered whether eventually one of these strangers would murder us in our beds. I have thought about starting a school to teach children French, leaving public health to work in renewable energy, volunteering with abuse shelters, and quite a few other things that never made it much beyond the idea stage.

This is just how I interact with the world. I read a book and want to try what it describes. I meet someone doing something interesting and wonder what I could learn from it. I see a problem and immediately begin thinking about how I would solve it. Some of these experiments have become important parts of my life. Some were interesting for a while and then stopped being interesting. Some were bad ideas. With most of them, the consequences were small enough that it did not matter very much. I learned something and moved on.

At work, this tendency has sometimes produced much larger results, which creates a danger of its own. Looking backward at Measurement & Improvement, for example, it is possible to construct a flattering story about what happened. I saw a problem nobody else understood, came up with an idea for measuring IIS, persuaded people to support it, and eventually watched that idea turn into a national program. There are pieces of truth in that version, but I no longer think it is the most accurate way to understand what happened.

Many people knew there was a problem. Many had ideas about measurement. I happened to be standing in a particularly useful place when those ideas were becoming important. I was working with IHS, I understood the IIS interfaces from the inside, and I had relationships across the community. I was willing to keep pulling on the thread, and other people recognized what I was talking about because some version of the same idea was already in their heads.

I was also lucky. I found receptive people. The timing was right. Federal policy was rapidly expanding electronic exchange. There was money available when the size of the problem became apparent. Later I joined an organization with colleagues who could turn an early technical effort into something much larger than I could have built myself. The idea that survived was not simply something I had invented and imposed on everyone else. It found fertile ground because it expressed something the community was ready to do.

That matters to me more now because people are more likely to listen to my ideas than they were twenty years ago. Success creates credibility, but it can also create a kind of survivorship bias. I can tell stories about the ideas that worked because those are the ones still around. I rarely give presentations about the dozens of things I advocated that went nowhere, were mistimed, lacked resources, or simply weren't very good. If I forget those, I can begin treating every new idea as though it is another great idea temporarily misunderstood by the world. That would be a terrible lesson to take from success.

Open source software is one example. I have advocated for it for years. It fits me extremely well philosophically. I like openness. I like giving things away. I like people being able to see how something works, reuse it, and improve it. At different points I thought open source might fundamentally change how government software was developed and maintained. We have produced useful open source software, and I still believe in it, but it has not been the transformation I imagined. Making the source code available does not solve procurement, staffing, maintenance, incentives, governance, or the simple fact that somebody still has to understand and care for the software. I have not abandoned open source, but I have stopped expecting the idea itself to solve problems that turned out to be much larger than software licensing.

The more useful lesson may be that I should have more ideas, not fewer, while becoming less attached to any particular one. I once learned a useful trick from people who did design work: when possible, bring two ideas instead of one. If you show someone one design, the conversation easily becomes a critique of your design, and it is difficult not to experience that as a critique of you. Put two substantially different approaches on the table and the conversation changes. Now everyone is comparing possibilities. One idea can lose without its author losing. A third idea may emerge from the comparison that is better than either original proposal.

My instinct when I get interested in something is to make it real quickly enough that I can see what is wrong with it. Usually I do that by writing software. Sometimes I write a document, create a website, make a diagram, or convene a meeting. I am a detailed thinker, and I often cannot understand a problem completely at the abstract level. I need to get down inside it. With Measurement & Improvement, the eventual AART system was roughly the third generation of software I had built around the problem. Each version taught me things that made the previous assumptions obsolete. From one perspective, I had written software and then thrown it away. From another, writing the software was how I learned what problem I was actually trying to solve.

This way of working can be uncomfortable in organizations that want to plan before they build. Government in particular has understandable reasons for avoiding failure. Money has to be accounted for, plans have to be defended, and leaders do not enjoy explaining why an organization spent time creating something that was later discarded. My instinct is often to push the other way. Give me enough room to make a small version of the thing and I will be able to tell you much more intelligently what the project plan should say. I do not know that I have completely solved the tension between those approaches. I probably also drive my leadership a little crazy with it.

What keeps the experimentation from being completely irresponsible is that I usually build around work I already have to do. I do not sit around writing software for theoretical problems belonging to someone else. If I am responsible for a difficult process and think there might be a better way to do it, I build something that helps me perform that process. It still has to accomplish today's work. The experiment is in whether I can also make the same work easier, faster, or better next year. Many experiments disappear, but they generally leave behind either a useful tool or a much clearer understanding of why that particular approach was wrong. Occasionally one survives and begins to grow.

Survival, however, requires something I used to find much more painful: other people changing what I created. An early version of AART produced reports that made perfect sense to me. I had built the software, understood the measures, and knew exactly what every piece of information meant. Other people looked at those reports and had trouble understanding them. We redesigned them, and then redesigned them again. By the time we had something that really worked, perhaps a quarter of the resulting design was recognizably mine and the rest had come from people who had criticized, questioned, and reworked it. The core idea was still there, but the useful expression of it belonged to the team.

I have come to appreciate the difference between criticism and indifference. If I send out a long document and nobody comments on it, that can feel pleasant. Nobody has told me I am wrong. It can also mean nobody found the document important enough to spend time on. When three busy colleagues return something with comments on nearly every page, the experience is less comfortable, but what they have actually done is spend several hours of their limited lives thinking seriously about something I created. Increasingly my response to that is genuine gratitude. I have been saved from an extraordinary number of bad statements and bad decisions by people willing to tell me where they thought I was wrong.

I once heard similar advice about a husband and wife building a household budget. Often one person does the work of creating the first budget, brings it to the other person, and then becomes defensive as the other person starts changing it. The advice was that the person who wrote the first draft should learn to be quiet for a while. They have already had a tremendous amount of influence. Their assumptions and preferences are embedded everywhere in the first version. Now the other person needs a chance to put themselves into it. Otherwise it is not really a shared budget at all. I think about collaborative work in much the same way now. Creating the first draft is my turn. If I want other people to own the result, I eventually have to let them have theirs.

That has become easier as I have accumulated other things I want to work on. Years ago, when someone took one of my ideas and began changing it, I could feel myself wince. Now I try to notice something else: this person likes the idea enough that they want it. It is almost like watching someone adopt a puppy I have been caring for. They are going to feed it differently, train it differently, perhaps even give it another name. But they want the puppy. That means I no longer have to be the person responsible for it, and I have plenty of other puppies.

Measurement & Improvement has reached something close to that point for me. I remain involved and I still care deeply about its long-term direction, but I am no longer responsible for its day-to-day operation. Very little of the work depends on me being available next Tuesday to make something happen. Other people have replaced me, developed specialties I do not have, changed processes I once designed, and taken the work in directions I would not necessarily have chosen myself. I love this. It means the work survived the transition from being something I did to being something the organization does.

Perhaps teaching is an even better analogy. A good kindergarten teacher can care intensely about the children in the classroom, but the goal is not to produce children who remain dependent on their kindergarten teacher. They leave. Other teachers influence them. They develop interests and abilities nobody could have predicted when they were five. One may eventually become a statesman; another may become a wonderfully kind grocery store clerk. The kindergarten teacher does not get to choose, and there would be something unhealthy about wanting to. You had an important responsibility for a period of time. Then someone else does. I am beginning to think ideas should be treated similarly. You can nurture them when they are fragile, advocate for them when nobody understands them, and give them enough structure to survive. Eventually, if they are healthy, they should outgrow you.

There is another change happening alongside this one. I think I need to talk less. In my twenties and thirties I talked constantly, and I can still fill a room with words if nobody stops me. What has changed is that I am less confident that my first response is what another person needs. Experience has shown me too many different ways of solving the same kind of problem. A child struggling to do something might need instruction, a hint, an example, encouragement, or simply enough space to fail once and try again. Any of those responses can be wise. Any can also be exactly the wrong response. You cannot determine which one is right from a rule. You have to pay attention to the child.

Professional work increasingly feels that way to me. Sometimes people need me to solve the technical problem. Sometimes they need me to explain something they do not understand. Sometimes they need a meeting organized or two people introduced. Sometimes they already know what to do and what they really need is someone to listen long enough for them to hear themselves say it. Occasionally the right thing is to disagree clearly. Occasionally it is to say nothing at all. I am more interested now in figuring out what level of intervention is useful than in demonstrating that I have something to contribute.

Paradoxically, this may be possible because I have more authority than I did when I was younger. I no longer feel quite the same pressure to establish myself by speaking quickly. I can stay with a problem longer, ask questions, and wait. When I finally contribute, people are more likely to listen because they have some history with me. I would like to use that privilege to make my contributions less frequent and more valuable, rather than simply becoming a louder version of myself.

That is also part of why I have become interested in working outside public health again. I am not trying to leave public health. It remains the place where I have spent most of my career, where many of the people I care about professionally work, and where I still have things I very much want to accomplish. But I have spent twenty years carrying ideas from software development, quality improvement, education, marketing, organizational design, and other fields into public health. I am curious what would happen if I widened that exchange. Could I be useful in another technical domain? What would I discover there that I could carry back?

The question matters because curiosity by itself is not enough. Another organization has no obligation to educate me simply because I find its problems fascinating. If I want access to a new field, I have to make the exchange worthwhile. In that sense it is much like our approach to measurement. We wanted IIS programs to participate because doing so would improve the whole community, but the program worked because participating also gave the individual IIS something it needed. If I want an organization in another field to let me close enough to understand its problems, I need to contribute enough value that having me there makes sense for them. The learning can be my benefit, but it cannot be my only contribution.

I am not entirely sure where that leads. That uncertainty is partly what interests me. I would like the next part of my career to contain more experiments, not simply larger versions of the projects I already know how to do. I want enough influence that when I see something promising, people are willing to let us try it. But I am increasingly aware that this is different from wanting enough influence to make everyone adopt my answer. I would rather create more possibilities, expose them to reality sooner, let weak ones disappear without too much grief, and let strong ones be changed by people who see things I cannot.

For years I thought my contribution was having ideas and convincing people to pursue them. I think the next stage may require something slightly different: listening long enough to recognize ideas that are already forming around me, giving them enough shape that people can react to them, and then knowing when to get out of the way.

I still want to make things happen. I just want to become less particular about whose idea they become.
